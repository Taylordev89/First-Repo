Do not use "reportedly" or "according to" inside the hook; place attribution in a nearby line.

HOOK OPENER: Prefer to open the hook with ONE of these patterns, and use one for most stories: "Have you heard about the" / "Imagine waking up and..." / "What would you do if" / "You've been told" / "How could a". Pick the one that fits the story's shape:

"Imagine waking up and...": when the story has a shocking discovery that happened to a person firsthand (they found something, they saw something).

"What would you do if...": when the story has a threat or a trapping situation.

"Have you heard about the...": when the story is about a named case, place, or thing.

"You've been told...": when the official version and the real truth are different.

"How could a...": when something seems impossible but happened.

Use the opener ONLY if the whole sentence passes this test: read it aloud once, and it must flow without stumbling, with the opener leading straight into the story's strongest concrete image/CORE IMAGE with no filler words, no awkward bridging, and no generic ending. If it does not pass, drop the opener and write the strongest natural hook for this story instead. A hook without an opener must be just as vivid and just as concrete. Never use an opener as decoration; the sentence after it must create the dread and curiosity.

The hook is ONE complete sentence, normally 12–25 words (a shorter hook of 6–11 words is allowed only if it is genuinely stronger). It is the ONLY line exempt from the line-length range in the VOICEOVER SCRIPT RULES.

The hook line must create INSTANT dread or curiosity. It must make the viewer feel something in 3 seconds.

The normal CAPITAL emphasis rules apply to the hook line (1–3 capitalized words).

HOOK GROUNDING: The hook must contain at least one concrete subject that a camera could literally photograph (an object, a body part, a creature, a place, an empty landscape) and that subject must be something the story itself actually shows, including again somewhere in script line 2 onward. Never pick an anchor just to satisfy this rule; choose the strongest real element of the story. Every physical person, object, place, or detail the hook names must come from the topic's facts or the established story. Never add a physical detail the story never shows (example: do not say "in your bed" if no bed ever appears). Abstract or emotional wording is welcome around that anchor when it strengthens the hook, but it must not introduce a new physical subject, location, or event. In second-person openers ("Imagine waking up...", "What would you do..."), "you" does not count as a person who must appear. Silently check this before finalizing. Do not print the check.

Body rules:

Write EXACTLY the number of lines from Step 1.

Every sentence must be highly visual and cinematic. Ensure the imagery flows naturally, as a single spoken long meaningful sentence may span across multiple visual scenes later.

Short. Cinematic. Punchy.

NEVER write journalistic or Wikipedia-style lines. Focus on cinematic flow and curiosity.

Example of Narrative Flow:

BAD (Boring, Report-Style):

Lisa arrived in Athens.

She moved into an apartment.

She made her final call.

Police examined the phone.

GOOD (Cinematic, Flowing, Dark Tone):

Lisa had come to Athens to help people who had nowhere else to GO.

Then, inside her new apartment, the trail suddenly went SILENT.

Example #2:

BAD: "Officials buried him inside layers of protocol."

GOOD: "Every guard who saw him was quietly reassigned, and SILENCED."

Emotional arc is MANDATORY:

Lines 1–30% → Hook and establish dread

Lines 30–70% → Build tension, reveal dark details.

Lines 70–100% → Escalate to maximum dread, then close.

NEVER let tension collapse in the middle section.

No meta-commentary. No academic summaries.

Stay inside the story at all times.

Tone: cold, slow-burn, dark documentary narrator.

Never theatrical. Never over-dramatic.

Quiet menace is more powerful than loud horror.

NO bold. NO italics. CAPITAL LETTERS for emphasis only —

Max 1-3 capitalized words per line; applied to the STRONGEST visual or emotional word.

Continuous Flow: The script should not sound like a list of disconnected, isolated headlines. Each sentence must naturally bridge into the next beat to keep the viewer hooked.

Closing line rules:

The final line must make the viewer's skin crawl and feel like the story is not over.

It must haunt them after they stop watching.

Build it from the story's own strongest unresolved fact, image, or question, something the facts really leave open. Never invent a present-day haunting, sound, or event that is not in the topic's facts.

BAD: "The mystery was never solved." GOOD: "The film survived, but the reason those three men died never did."

Before finalizing, silently check every line: ends with . ? or !, 8–16 words except the 2 or 3 punch lines of 4–7 words (hook exempt), 1–3 CAPITALIZED words. Rewrite any line that fails. Do not print this check.

STEP 4 — VISUAL BEAT MAPPING (MENTAL STEP FOR SCENE MAP)

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ HOOK MAPPING (always done FIRST, separately from the story mapping)

The hook (script line 1) is mapped by PHRASE — not by sentence, and not by the 3-second beat rule. The hook is one sentence, but it still gets one visual per phrase.

Copy the hook line exactly and split it, in order, without changing or dropping any word, into natural phrases. Split based on natural storytelling and distinct visual beats. Do NOT split a single continuous visual action, complete thought, or prepositional phrase just to create more phrases. Natural visual logic and meaning ALWAYS matter more than strict word counts. Let the natural meaning and visual rhythm dictate the splits. A phrase shorter than 3 words merges into its neighbor. A hook that is a single short phrase (8 words or fewer, no natural break) gets exactly ONE visual.
Example 1 (opener with nothing to photograph): HOOK — "Have you heard about the Scottish house where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?"

Bad: [H1] - "Have you heard about the" [H2] - "Scottish house where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?" Why: H1 names nothing a camera can photograph. Good: [H1] - "Have you heard about the Scottish house" [H2] - "where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?"

Example 2 (witness lead-in): HOOK — "Imagine waking up and seeing a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW, twenty miles from any HELP."

Bad: [H1] - "Imagine waking up and seeing a dead man's ARM" [H2] - "waving at your WINDOW," [H3] - "twenty miles from any HELP." Good: [H1] - "Imagine waking up and seeing" [H2] - "a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW," [H3] - "twenty miles from any HELP." Why: this story shows the surviving keeper who saw the arm, so H1 shows him noticing, and H2 keeps the complete continuous action ("a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW") intact in one phrase. If the story had no real witness, "Imagine waking up and seeing" would merge directly into H2 as a single phrase.

Example 3 (keeping a continuous state together vs. splitting two distinct images): HOOK — "How could a single roll of FILM sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years, and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?"

Bad (splits one continuous state into two repetitive shots of the same canister): [H1] - "How could a single roll of FILM" [H2] - "sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years," [H3] - "and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?" Good (keeps the frozen film canister as one complete thought, then shifts to the developed photograph): [H1] - "How could a single roll of FILM sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years," [H2] - "and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?" Why: H1 is one continuous physical state (the film canister trapped in Arctic ice), and H2 is the contrasting visual reveal (the developed photograph showing three living men).

Each phrase gets exactly ONE visual, labeled H1, H2, H3… The hook never gets more or fewer visuals than it has phrases.

For every phrase, write: what it literally says, the one concrete image that shows it, its SUBJECT LOCK, its SOURCE, and its FALLBACK.

SUBJECT LOCK = five facts in one line: main subject — exact count (arms, hands, people, objects) — what it is attached to or resting on — where it sits in the frame (and camera viewpoint if a window/door/barrier is present) — ACTION CUE (the single frozen-frame pose that shows the phrase's action word, per the ACTION-VERB RULE in Step 5; write "none" if the phrase has no action word).

Example #1: "stranger's hand — 1 hand — attached to a coat sleeve entering from the bottom edge — center of frame — WAVING: palm out, fingers spread, wrist tilted, slight blur on the fingertips, touching nothing" Example #2: "wooden rocking chair — 1 chair — standing on a bare attic floor — center of frame — ROCKING: chair tilted mid-swing on its curved runners, one runner lifted slightly off the floor, nobody sitting in it”.

SOURCE:
OPTION 1 — ALL NEW (DEFAULT PRIORITY): For every hook phrase, always prioritize creating a brand-new, hyper-scroll-stopping visual that represents that specific hook phrase and the story's CORE IMAGE in the strongest possible way. Use OPTION 1 whenever every hook phrase can be shown more powerfully with a dedicated new visual.
OPTION 2 — MIX: Most Hook Visuals NEW, One REUSED from the Story (ONLY THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT EXACT-MATCH SCENE REUSED FROM THE STORY): Only reuse a visual from the story for a hook phrase (not for every hook phrase, ONLY for the most important/climactic one) when ALL of the following conditions are met
No Stronger New Visual Possible: For that specific hook phrase, it is not possible to create a NEW visual that is stronger or better than the scene already present in the story.

Core Event Alignment: That specific hook phrase directly refers to the CORE IMAGE or the exact same main/climactic event shown later in the story.

Strict Exact-Match Condition: The visual MUST be reused ONLY from the exact story beat (S#) where the narration describes the exact same moment and physical action spoken in the hook phrase (e.g., if the hook phrase says "a dead keeper's ARM waving at the lighthouse WINDOW," it can ONLY be reused from the exact story beat where the corpse's arm moves against the window "as if WAVING"—never from an unrelated window shot or a coffin shot).

No Forced Reuse: If the exact same scene and action do not exist later in the story, forcing a reuse is strictly forbidden—you MUST create a NEW visual for that hook phrase instead (Option 1). Never reuse visuals for every hook phrase; only reuse the single most important exact-match beat. • A REUSED visual is fully written at its hook position. When the story mapping reaches that same moment, the story prompt is a 100% FULL-TEXT copy of that hook prompt (every element written out in full, word for word, never shortened and never replaced by 'same as before' or '[REUSE]'). In the VISUAL SCENE MAP, mark that story beat with '[same as H#]' at the end of its line, and in the VIDEO PROMPTS code block, keep the standard clean header 'STORY PROMPT S#:' followed by the full-text copy so automated image generation and file naming never break.

FALLBACK: for EVERY hook visual (NEW or REUSE), name the script line (line number and its first few words) where the story shows the same moment. This isn't an extra prompt or image—that story visual is generated anyway. It exists so the editor, after seeing the generated images, can swap it in if the hook image fails. If the story never shows that moment, write "none".

OPENER MAPPING: Decide by content, not by length. An opener is a framing phrase, not a scene. "Have you heard about the", "What would you do if", "You've been told" and "How could a" never get their own visual; each joins the first concrete image that follows it. Only a lead-in that describes a real person's action ("Imagine waking up and seeing") may get its own visual, and only if the story shows that person (the witness, survivor, or finder) in the story's own location. If the story has no such person, it joins the next phrase like any other opener. Never invent a generic person, bed, or room for a lead-in. Use the story's own witness, location, or object.

A reaction visual is allowed only when the very next hook visual shows what is being reacted to.

Exception to rule 1 (VERY LONG SINGLE-ACTION HOOK PHRASES ONLY): Do NOT split a continuous action of 9 to 14 words (such as Example 3's H1). ONLY if a single continuous action clause in the hook is 15 or more words AND cannot be read clearly in one shot, split it into an establishing shot and an extreme close-up detail of the SAME subject and location—never invent a second unrelated subject, and never show the same shot size twice.

Story mapping starts at script line 2 and uses the 3-second beat and STORY MAPPING rules below. Never map the hook line again in the story mapping.

The hook's CORE IMAGE must be fully visible in at least one hook visual. Never keep it off-camera or hidden.

Second-person hooks ("your window", "at you"): if the phrase has no real witness from the story (or describes what the witness saw, like H2 in Example 2), show the scene directly from the viewer's/witness's point of view with no invented person. Only a lead-in phrase that describes a real witness's own action (like H1 in Example 2, per Rule 7) may show that real witness. Never add a character the story never shows.

STORY MAPPING (script line 2 onward)

DO NOT rewrite or alter the voiceover script from Step 3. The spoken script MUST remain in complete, unbroken sentences, ending with proper punctuation.

Instead, mentally break down those complete sentences into ~3-second visual beats. Hook visuals are labeled [H1], [H2]… and story visuals are labeled [S1], [S2]… (two separate numberings).

You will output this breakdown in the "VISUAL SCENE MAP" in the Final Output (the hook breakdown is also printed in the HOOK BREAKDOWN section).

How to map it (MEANING-FIRST BEAT SLICING — NO DEAD FRAGMENTS):

SINGLE-ACTION SHORT/MEDIUM SENTENCES (2 to 10 words = ONE BEAT):
If a complete sentence from Step 3 is 2 to 10 words AND describes ONE single continuous action or visual state, map the ENTIRE sentence to ONE single beat [S#].
NEVER slice a 9- or 10-word sentence if slicing it would leave a "dead prepositional tail" (such as "...inside the freezing stone cell", "...for thirty-three years", or "...across the empty floor") that has no distinct visual action or new subject of its own.
Example (Short/Single-Action Sentence — Do NOT slice): Spoken Sentence: "The city went SILENT." Mapped into Visual Beat: [S1] - "The city went SILENT."

Spoken Sentence: "One monk knelt alone inside the freezing stone CELL." (9 words, 1 single action) Mapped into Visual Beat: [S2] - "One monk knelt alone inside the freezing stone CELL."

LONGER OR TWO-PART SENTENCES (11 to 16 words, or 9–10 words with TWO distinct actions = TWO BEATS):
Split sentences of 11 to 16 words (or 9–10 word sentences that contain two separate actions/clauses) into TWO beats at the most natural clause break, so each beat is roughly 4 to 8 words.
SCRIPT-FIRST ANTI-REPETITION SLICING RULE: Whenever you split one sentence into two beats ([S1] and [S2]), both beats must stay 100% faithful to the sentence's actual words (never invent unmentioned objects or marks), while never producing two identical camera shots: • If the sentence has TWO actions or subjects, Beat 1 shows the first action/subject, and Beat 2 shows the second action/subject. • If a long 11–16 word sentence describes ONE continuous action or state, keep the true subject from the script in both beats, but change the camera scale and angle: Beat 1 shows the main subject and action in a Wide or Medium shot, and Beat 2 shifts the CUE to a tight Close-Up or alternate angle of a specific part of that SAME subject, body, or setting actually named or implied by the sentence—NEVER repeat the identical framing/pose, NEVER invent props not in the story, and NEVER show a blank wall or empty landscape!
LONGER OR TWO-PART SENTENCES (11 to 16 words, or 9–10 words with TWO distinct actions = TWO BEATS):
Split sentences of 11 to 16 words (or 9–10 word sentences that contain two separate actions/clauses) into TWO beats at the most natural clause break, so each beat is roughly 4 to 8 words.
CRITICAL ANTI-REPETITION SLICING RULE: Whenever you split one sentence into two beats ([S1] and [S2]), the two beats must NEVER produce the same camera shot or an empty filler background: • If the sentence has TWO actions/subjects, Beat 1 shows the first action/subject, and Beat 2 shows the second action/subject. • If a long 11–16 word sentence describes ONE continuous scene in the same location, Beat 1 shows the Main Subject & Action (Medium or Wide shot), and Beat 2 MUST shift the CUE to a Distinct Camera Angle, Extreme Close-Up Detail, or Physical Consequence in that same room/location (e.g., close-up of the trembling hands, the tool on the table, or the scratch marks)—NEVER repeat the same pose/framing, and NEVER show a blank wall or empty landscape!
STRICT CUE GROUNDING RULE: Every CUE is at most 12 words naming ONLY the beat's subject and its one frozen pose or key noun. A CUE may ONLY use subjects, body parts, objects, or surfaces explicitly named in that beat's spoken words or the immediately preceding beat's spoken words in the same continuous sentence, the CHARACTER SHEET, or STORY WORLD—NEVER invent extra props or details (such as an unmentioned bag, paper, or cracked screen, etc).

Example Mapping Process (Two-Beat Sentence — Zero Invented Props): Spoken Sentence (From Step 3): "Lisa had come to Athens to help people who had nowhere else to GO." Mapped into Visual Beats (For the Visual Scene Map): [S1] - "Lisa had come to Athens to help people..." | CUE: Lisa walking down a narrow Athens street in daylight [S2] - "...who had nowhere else to GO." | CUE: Close shot of Lisa speaking gently to a huddled group

Style & Pacing Guidelines:

~3-Second Beats: Break the text down into natural beats that take roughly 1.5 to 3 seconds to narrate.

Continuous Flow: The script should not sound like a list of disconnected, isolated headlines. Each sentence must naturally bridge into the next beat to keep the viewer hooked.

Dynamic Visual Progression (Spatial Continuity): Every single beat must receive a unique visual prompt, but do NOT randomly jump locations in the middle of a continuous sentence. CRITICAL: Even when maintaining the same location across multiple beats, you MUST still write out the full environment, lighting, and physics descriptions in every single prompt, exactly as required in Step 5. Never write "same as before."

Example of the Desired Output (Story Mapping from Line 2 onward with CUEs): [S1] - "For days, messages reportedly came from her NUMBER." | CUE: Lit smartphone screen glowing in a dim room [S2] - "Then one contact noticed something felt WRONG." | CUE: Contact noticing something on the phone [S3] - "The replies no longer sounded like Lisa." | CUE: Extreme close-up of text message bubbles on the phone screen [S4] - "And miles away, a heavy suitcase was WAITING." | CUE: Heavy closed suitcase sitting alone on the ground

STEP 5 — GENERATE VISUALS PROMPTS

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ These prompts make STILL IMAGES. In editing, each image gets a slow zoom or pan keyframe and stays on screen while its beat is spoken. Each image is generated alone, by a model that has never seen the script or any other prompt, so every prompt must be complete on its own.

Before generating the image prompts, you must mentally map the Voiceover Script into visual beats using the HOOK MAPPING and STORY MAPPING rules given in Step 4. You will output this breakdown in the FINAL OUTPUT as the 'HOOK BREAKDOWN' and the 'VISUAL SCENE MAP'.

For each mapped visual moment, write one image prompt, built from its SUBJECT LOCK (hook) or its CUE (story).

⚠️ CRITICAL MAPPING RULE: Before writing each prompt, ask: "What is LITERALLY happening in this exact beat RIGHT NOW?" That answer = the visual. NOT what happened before. NOT what comes next. ONLY what this exact beat's SUBJECT LOCK (for hook) or CUE (for story) describes. Never skip. Never combine. Never reorder.

⚠️ ACTION-VERB RULE (applies to EVERY prompt — hook and story): Find the action word or key noun of the beat (waving, tapping, dragging, staring, burying, falling, a pale arm, a locked door…). The image must show EXACTLY that action — never a nearby, calmer, or different action. A still frame shows an action through ONE frozen pose, so before writing the prompt, choose the pose that can only mean that verb, for example: • WAVING = hand raised, open palm facing the target, fingers spread, wrist tilted off vertical, slight blur on the fingertips — NOT pressed against, resting on, or touching anything. • TAPPING = a knuckle or fingertip just making contact with the surface. • DRAGGING = the body or object leaning against the pull, with a visible trail behind it. • RUNNING / FALLING / REACHING / POINTING / any other verb = the unmistakable frozen pose of that verb. The frozen pose must be in a form this subject can physically do (a lifeless body or corpse cannot act on its own and moves only by wind, water, or gravity). If the beat has no action word, the image shows its key noun large and clear. Never replace the beat's action with a resting or neutral pose. Write every prompt from its SUBJECT LOCK (for hook) or its CUE line (for story).

⚠️ CRITICAL TEXT RULE: Do NOT render any text, subtitles, captions, split-screen format, watermarks, or words inside the image. The image must be completely clean. If a story object itself carries letters or numbers (a carved name, tacks spelling initials), keep them small or out of focus.

CHARACTER SHEET (printed in the Final Output): for every person who appears, one fixed CHARACTER TAG of 12–20 words (age, build, skin tone, face, hair colour and length, clothing colour and fabric), taken from the topic's facts or, where the facts are silent, neutral period-accurate choices (used only in the images, never stated in the narration). Copy the tag word for word into every prompt that shows that person; change only what the story changes (e.g. "now gaunt, beard grown"). Face, hair and build never change. If a scene needs different clothing (indoors, at night), write that person's second tag once in the CHARACTER SHEET and copy it word for word in those scenes. Unnamed groups or crowds (e.g., "villagers", "search party", "passengers") do not get individual tags in the CHARACTER SHEET; describe them collectively in the beat with period-accurate clothing. Never use a name alone.

STORY WORLD (printed in the Final Output): 3–5 short facts taken only from the topic — era, location layout (what is inside, outside, above, below), and key props with the script line from which each exists or changes (line number and its first few words, e.g. "from line 6 ('By morning the trunk…'): the trunk sits sealed in the cellar"). Use a fact only in beats where it applies; a prop appears only when its beat needs it. Keep these facts to at most 5 short lines. LOCATION TAGS (part of STORY WORLD): for every specific setting that appears in 3 or more beats, create a fixed LOCATION TAG of 12–20 words. Keep EXTERIOR and INTERIOR tags separate so exterior roofs/landscapes never leak into interior shots:

For an EXTERIOR setting: building/structure shape, exterior wall material and colour, roof style, and immediate terrain/surroundings.
For an INTERIOR setting: room scale, wall and floor material/colour, key fixed architectural feature (e.g., narrow barred window, stone hearth), and ambient lighting tone. Copy the matching INTERIOR or EXTERIOR tag word for word into the [Environment] section of every Wide or Medium prompt set in that space. In Extreme Close-Up or Macro shots where the room or building is out of frame, do NOT paste the full room/building layout—use only the immediate contact surface and the tag's lighting and wall/surface colour tone so the camera stays tight on the close-up subject.
⚠️ HOOK VISUAL RULES — apply to H1…Hn only:

STRICT PHRASE COUNT: One prompt per hook phrase — never merge two phrases, never skip a phrase, never add an extra hook prompt.
LITERAL PHRASE ACCURACY: Before writing each hook prompt, re-read the FULL hook line, then that one phrase. The image must LITERALLY show what that phrase says — same subject, same action, same moment. The action word must be visible as its frozen pose (ACTION-VERB RULE) that can only mean that verb, in a form this subject can physically do (a lifeless body moves only by wind, water, or gravity). Never swap it for a calmer or nearby action.
SCROLL-STOPPING & CORE IMAGE LOCK: Hook visuals must be the strongest, most disturbing, mysterious, or curious images their phrases allow, and together they must cover the story's CORE IMAGE. The CORE IMAGE must be fully visible in at least one hook image (never off-camera or hidden). H1 must be the single most scroll-stopping image its phrase allows. A reaction image is allowed ONLY if the very next hook image shows what is being reacted to. All hook images must look like frames of the same reality.
ONE MAIN SUBJECT & CENTRAL FRAMING: ONE main subject, large in the central 70% of the frame and readable in one second; state in plain words where it sits in the frame.
RENDER-SAFE & VIEWPOINT CLARITY: Use physical arrangements an image generator has seen thousands of times (a coat hanging over a chair, a hand raised in front of a window, a small house alone in a field). Only ONE unusual element per image; everything else ordinary. Whenever glass, a window, a door, or water separates things, explicitly state where the camera is and which side every subject is on. Never describe a body part passing through gaps, holes, or planks; never "emerging from between" things; never place an object in an unusual orientation (e.g., a bed standing on its end); never use "swallowed by darkness".
ZERO CLUTTER: Name nothing the phrase does not need. Any extra object you mention (a lamp, a rope, a bird) will be drawn prominently — leave it out unless it is the subject.
STRICT ANATOMY: Every visible body part is attached to a visible sleeve or body, or enters the frame cleanly from an edge; state exact counts (hands, arms, people); the rest of the body stays outside the frame.
NO FILLER VISUALS: No isolated "character reacting to something off-camera" (a reaction shot is only allowed if the very next hook visual shows what they saw), no atmosphere-only shots, no hiding the subject, and no invented faces outside the CHARACTER SHEET. Dread comes from WHAT is shown.
FORMAT PARITY: Hook prompts use the exact same element order and Technical tail as story prompts.
⚠️ STORY VISUAL RULES — apply to S1…Sm ONLY:

FORWARD NARRATIVE PROGRESSION (NO CORE IMAGE BLEED): Story prompts must visually advance the narrative chronologically from beat to beat. Do NOT stay stuck on the Hook's CORE IMAGE across the story; only show the CORE IMAGE in the specific story beat(s) where the narration actually reaches that exact object or event. Every story beat must show what is happening in THAT exact beat right now.
SCRIPT-FIRST ANTI-REPETITION & SHOT-SCALE PROGRESSION (CRITICAL):
OBEY THE SCRIPT FIRST, VARY THE CAMERA SECOND: Always show the exact character, object, or place named by the beat's words—never replace the script's subject with an invented subject just to force variety. Instead, prevent visual repetition through camera scale, angle, and sentence phrasing:
NO DUPLICATE OPENINGS OR IDENTICAL FRAMING: Even when the same character, body, or object appears in multiple story beats across the script, NEVER start two different prompts with the exact same subject-verb opening or show the exact same camera framing twice (e.g., if an earlier beat already used a medium shot starting with "One monk kneels on the floor...", a later beat involving that kneeling monk must keep him kneeling as the script requires, but MUST start with the new focal detail and shift to a tight close-up of his hands, quill, or gaunt face, or a high/over-the-shoulder angle).
NO REPEATED WIDE EXTERIOR PANORAMAS: Show at most ONE wide exterior establishing shot of the same village, building, or landscape. If a later beat names that same settlement or building again, show it through a Medium or Close-Up architectural/environmental detail (e.g., a single snow-drifted doorway, a shuttered window, or a frost-covered stone wall) unless the beat explicitly describes a new wide-scale event affecting the whole site.
CONSECUTIVE BEATS OF THE SAME SUBJECT OR ACTION: When two consecutive story beats (S# and S#+1) involve the same subject, character, or continuous action, keep the same CHARACTER TAG, LOCATION TAG, and prop state, BUT you MUST change BOTH the shot size AND camera angle between S# and S#+1 (e.g., Medium Shot -> Extreme Close-Up).
ANTI-REPETITION & SHOT-SCALE PROGRESSION (CRITICAL):
NEVER start two different story prompts with the exact same subject-verb opening or identical physical pose (for example, never write "One monk kneels..." or "An empty settlement..." in two separate story prompts).
NEVER stack multiple wide exterior or landscape shots of the same village, building, or terrain across the script.
CONSECUTIVE BEATS OF ONE CONTINUOUS ACTION: When two consecutive story beats (S# and S#+1) show the same continuous action or split one sentence in the same location, keep the same CHARACTER TAG, LOCATION TAG, and prop state, BUT you MUST change BOTH the shot size AND camera angle (e.g., if S1 is a medium or wide shot of the person acting, S2 MUST shift to an extreme close-up of the hands, the object, or a low/over-the-shoulder angle).
NON-CONTINUOUS CONSECUTIVE BEATS: Two consecutive story beats that are NOT one continuous action must NEVER show the same type of subject or framing twice in a row.
ONE MAIN SUBJECT & ZERO CLUTTER: Each story prompt must feature ONE clear focal subject (a pair or group named by the beat counts as one subject), large and readable in one second. Name nothing the beat does not need; any extra prop you mention will be drawn prominently.
VIEWPOINT & BARRIER CLARITY: Whenever glass, a window, a doorway, a wall, or water separates elements in a scene, explicitly state where the camera is positioned and which side of the barrier every subject is on (e.g., "camera outside in the dark street; the woman stands inside, behind the lit shop window").
STRICT ANATOMY & PHYSICAL GROUNDING: State exact counts (1 hand, 2 hands, 1 body). - Do not convert every input bullet into one narration line
- Never invent specific events, quotes, or physical details that are not in the topic's facts. The closing line may be atmospheric, but it must not assert a new factual event.

VOICEOVER SCRIPT RULES (STRICT COMPLETION):

The final script MUST be written for seamless AI voiceover generation.

Therefore, you must NEVER break a single meaningful sentence across multiple lines.

- One Complete Sentence = One Line
- Every single line MUST end with a full stop (.), question mark (?), or exclamation mark (!).
- Keep sentences short, punchy, and cinematic: most lines 8 to 16 words, and 2 or 3 punch lines of 4 to 7 words placed where the tension peaks. Do not write every line at the same length. The hook is the only line exempt from this range. 

Hook rules:

- The HOOK is script line 1 ONLY — the very first line of the script. Everything from line 2 onward is the story. Never treat line 2 or any later line as part of the hook.

- CORE IMAGE: Before writing the hook, pick the story's CORE IMAGE: the most disturbing, mysterious, or curious thing that was actually seen or found in the story (or, if the mystery is a sound, the physical object, barrier, or place where that sound occurred), in its simplest photographable form (one main subject in one ordinary physical arrangement). A belief, rumor, or reputation is not a core image. The hook must contain the core image, and it must be the concrete anchor required by HOOK GROUNDING. Do not use "reportedly" or "according to" inside the hook; place attribution in a nearby line.

- HOOK OPENER: Prefer to open the hook with ONE of these patterns, and use one for most stories: "Have you heard about the" / "Imagine waking up and..." / "What would you do if" / "You've been told" / "How could a". Pick the one that fits the story's shape:

"Imagine waking up and...": when the story has a shocking discovery that happened to a person firsthand (they found something, they saw something).

"What would you do if...": when the story has a threat or a trapping situation.

"Have you heard about the...": when the story is about a named case, place, or thing.

"You've been told...": when the official version and the real truth are different.

"How could a...": when something seems impossible but happened.

Use the opener ONLY if the whole sentence passes this test: read it aloud once, and it must flow without stumbling, with the opener leading straight into the story's strongest concrete image/CORE IMAGE with no filler words, no awkward bridging, and no generic ending. If it does not pass, drop the opener and write the strongest natural hook for this story instead. A hook without an opener must be just as vivid and just as concrete. Never use an opener as decoration; the sentence after it must create the dread and curiosity.

- The hook is ONE complete sentence, normally 12–25 words (a shorter hook of 6–11 words is allowed only if it is genuinely stronger). It is the ONLY line exempt from the line-length range in the VOICEOVER SCRIPT RULES. 

- The hook line must create INSTANT dread or curiosity. It must make the viewer feel something in 3 seconds.

- The normal CAPITAL emphasis rules apply to the hook line (1–3 capitalized words).

- HOOK GROUNDING: The hook must contain at least one concrete subject that a camera could literally photograph (an object, a body part, a creature, a place, an empty landscape) and that subject must be something the story itself actually shows, including again somewhere in script line 2 onward. Never pick an anchor just to satisfy this rule; choose the strongest real element of the story. Every physical person, object, place, or detail the hook names must come from the topic's facts or the established story. Never add a physical detail the story never shows (example: do not say "in your bed" if no bed ever appears). Abstract or emotional wording is welcome around that anchor when it strengthens the hook, but it must not introduce a new physical subject, location, or event. In second-person openers ("Imagine waking up...", "What would you do..."), "you" does not count as a person who must appear. Silently check this before finalizing. Do not print the check.

Body rules:

- Write EXACTLY the number of lines from Step 1.

- Every sentence must be highly visual and cinematic. Ensure the imagery flows naturally, as a single spoken long meaningful sentence may span across multiple visual scenes later.

- VISUAL PROGRESSION: Every line must bring at least one new photographable element from the topic's facts (a new person, place, object, action, or physical consequence). Never let more than 2 consecutive lines center on the same unchanged object or subject; if the facts keep the story on one subject longer, each extra line must show a new physical change to it, never the same state again.


- Short. Cinematic. Punchy.

- NEVER write journalistic or Wikipedia-style lines. Focus on cinematic flow and curiosity.

Example of Narrative Flow:

  BAD (Boring, Report-Style): 

  Lisa arrived in Athens.

  She moved into an apartment.

  She made her final call.

  Police examined the phone.

  GOOD (Cinematic, Flowing, Dark Tone):

  Lisa had come to Athens to help people who had nowhere else to GO.

  Then, inside her new apartment, the trail suddenly went SILENT.

Example #2:

  BAD:  "Officials buried him inside layers of protocol."

  GOOD: "Every guard who saw him was quietly reassigned, and SILENCED."

- Emotional arc is MANDATORY:

  Lines 1–30%   → Hook and establish dread

  Lines 30–70%  → Build tension, reveal dark details.

  Lines 70–100% → Escalate to maximum dread, then close.

- NEVER let tension collapse in the middle section.

  No meta-commentary. No academic summaries. 

  Stay inside the story at all times.

- Tone: cold, slow-burn, dark documentary narrator.

  Never theatrical. Never over-dramatic.

  Quiet menace is more powerful than loud horror.

- NO bold. NO italics. CAPITAL LETTERS for emphasis only — 

Max 1-3 capitalized words per line; applied to the STRONGEST visual or emotional word.


Continuous Flow: The script should not sound like a list of disconnected, isolated headlines. Each sentence must naturally bridge into the next beat to keep the viewer hooked.

Closing line rules:

- The final line must make the viewer's skin crawl and feel like the story is not over.

- It must haunt them after they stop watching.

- Build it from the story's own strongest unresolved fact, image, or question, something the facts really leave open. Never invent a present-day haunting, sound, or event that is not in the topic's facts.

   BAD:  "The mystery was never solved."
   GOOD: "The film survived, but the reason those three men died never did."

Before finalizing, silently check every line: ends with . ? or !, 8–16 words except the 2 or 3 punch lines of 4–7 words (hook exempt), 1–3 CAPITALIZED words, and no 3 consecutive lines centered on the same unchanged subject. Rewrite any line that fails. Do not print this check.

HOOK MAPPING (always done FIRST, separately from the story mapping)
 
The hook (script line 1) is mapped by PHRASE — not by sentence, and not by the 3-second beat rule. The hook is one sentence, but it still gets one visual per phrase.
 
1. Copy the hook line exactly and split it, in order, without changing or dropping any word, into natural phrases. Split based on natural storytelling and distinct visual beats. Do NOT split a single continuous visual action, complete thought, or prepositional phrase just to create more phrases. Natural visual logic and meaning ALWAYS matter more than strict word counts. Let the natural meaning and visual rhythm dictate the splits. A phrase shorter than 3 words merges into its neighbor. A hook that is a single short phrase (8 words or fewer, no natural break) gets exactly ONE visual. 

Example 1 (opener with nothing to photograph):
HOOK — "Have you heard about the Scottish house where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?"


Bad:
[H1] - "Have you heard about the"
[H2] - "Scottish house where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?"
Why: H1 names nothing a camera can photograph.
Good:
[H1] - "Have you heard about the Scottish house"
[H2] - "where something unseen kept SNIFFING at closed DOORS?"

Example 2 (witness lead-in):
HOOK — "Imagine waking up and seeing a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW, twenty miles from any HELP."

Bad:
[H1] - "Imagine waking up and seeing a dead man's ARM"
[H2] - "waving at your WINDOW,"
[H3] - "twenty miles from any HELP."
Good:
[H1] - "Imagine waking up and seeing"
[H2] - "a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW,"
[H3] - "twenty miles from any HELP."
Why: this story shows the surviving keeper who saw the arm, so H1 shows him noticing, and H2 keeps the complete continuous action ("a dead man's ARM waving at your WINDOW") intact in one phrase. If the story had no real witness, "Imagine waking up and seeing" would merge directly into H2 as a single phrase.

Example 3 (keeping a continuous state together vs. splitting two distinct images):
HOOK — "How could a single roll of FILM sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years, and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?"

Bad (splits one continuous state into two repetitive shots of the same canister):
[H1] - "How could a single roll of FILM"
[H2] - "sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years,"
[H3] - "and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?"
Good (keeps the frozen film canister as one complete thought, then shifts to the developed photograph):
[H1] - "How could a single roll of FILM sit frozen in the Arctic for thirty-three years,"
[H2] - "and still show three men perfectly ALIVE?"
Why: H1 is one continuous physical state (the film canister trapped in Arctic ice), and H2 is the contrasting visual reveal (the developed photograph showing three living men).

2. Each phrase gets exactly ONE visual, labeled H1, H2, H3… The hook never gets more or fewer visuals than it has phrases.


3. For every phrase, write: what it literally says, the one concrete image that shows it, its SUBJECT LOCK, its SOURCE, and its FALLBACK.

4. SUBJECT LOCK = five facts in one line: main subject — exact count (arms, hands, people, objects) — what it is attached to or resting on — where it sits in the frame (and camera viewpoint if a window/door/barrier is present) — ACTION CUE (the single frozen-frame pose that shows the phrase's action word, per the ACTION-VERB RULE in Step 5; write "none" if the phrase has no action word).


Example #1: "stranger's hand — 1 hand — attached to a coat sleeve entering from the bottom edge — center of frame — WAVING: palm out, fingers spread, wrist tilted, slight blur on the fingertips, touching nothing"
Example #2: "wooden rocking chair — 1 chair — standing on a bare attic floor — center of frame — ROCKING: chair tilted mid-swing on its curved runners, one runner lifted slightly off the floor, nobody sitting in it”.

5. SOURCE:
- OPTION 1 — ALL NEW (DEFAULT PRIORITY): For every hook phrase, always prioritize creating a brand-new, hyper-scroll-stopping visual that represents that specific hook phrase and the story's CORE IMAGE in the strongest possible way. Use OPTION 1 whenever every hook phrase can be shown more powerfully with a dedicated new visual.
- OPTION 2 — MIX: Most Hook Visuals NEW, One REUSED from the Story (ONLY THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT EXACT-MATCH SCENE REUSED FROM THE STORY): 
Only reuse a visual from the story for a hook phrase (not for every hook phrase, ONLY for the most important/climactic one) when ALL of the following conditions are met
1. No Stronger New Visual Possible: For that specific hook phrase, it is not possible to create a NEW visual that is stronger or better than the scene already present in the story. 
2. Core Event Alignment: That specific hook phrase directly refers to the CORE IMAGE or the exact same main/climactic event shown later in the story. 
3. Strict Exact-Match Condition: The visual MUST be reused ONLY from the exact story beat (S#) where the narration describes the exact same moment and physical action spoken in the hook phrase (e.g., if the hook phrase says "a dead keeper's ARM waving at the lighthouse WINDOW," it can ONLY be reused from the exact story beat where the corpse's arm moves against the window "as if WAVING"—never from an unrelated window shot or a coffin shot). 
4. No Forced Reuse: If the exact same scene and action do not exist later in the story, forcing a reuse is strictly forbidden—you MUST create a NEW visual for that hook phrase instead (Option 1). Never reuse visuals for every hook phrase; only reuse the single most important exact-match beat. 
• A REUSED visual is fully written at its hook position. When the story mapping reaches that same moment, the story prompt is a 100% FULL-TEXT copy of that hook prompt (every element written out in full, word for word, never shortened and never replaced by 'same as before' or '[REUSE]'). In the VISUAL SCENE MAP, mark that story beat with '[same as H#]' at the end of its line, and in the VIDEO PROMPTS code block, keep the standard clean header 'STORY PROMPT S#:' followed by the full-text copy so automated image generation and file naming never break.

6. FALLBACK: for EVERY hook visual (NEW or REUSE), name the script line (line number and its first few words) where the story shows the same moment. This isn't an extra prompt or image—that story visual is generated anyway. It exists so the editor, after seeing the generated images, can swap it in if the hook image fails. If the story never shows that moment, write "none".

7. OPENER MAPPING: Decide by content, not by length. An opener is a framing phrase, not a scene. "Have you heard about the", "What would you do if", "You've been told" and "How could a" never get their own visual; each joins the first concrete image that follows it. Only a lead-in that describes a real person's action ("Imagine waking up and seeing") may get its own visual, and only if the story shows that person (the witness, survivor, or finder) in the story's own location. If the story has no such person, it joins the next phrase like any other opener. Never invent a generic person, bed, or room for a lead-in. Use the story's own witness, location, or object.  

8. A reaction visual is allowed only when the very next hook visual shows what is being reacted to.

9. Exception to rule 1 (VERY LONG SINGLE-ACTION HOOK PHRASES ONLY): Do NOT split a continuous action of 9 to 14 words (such as Example 3's H1). ONLY if a single continuous action clause in the hook is 15 or more words AND cannot be read clearly in one shot, split it into an establishing shot and an extreme close-up detail of the SAME subject and location—never invent a second unrelated subject, and never show the same shot size twice.


10. Story mapping starts at script line 2 and uses the 3-second beat and STORY MAPPING rules below. Never map the hook line again in the story mapping.

11. The hook's CORE IMAGE must be fully visible in at least one hook visual. Never keep it off-camera or hidden.

12. Second-person hooks ("your window", "at you"): if the phrase has no real witness from the story (or describes what the witness saw, like H2 in Example 2), show the scene directly from the viewer's/witness's point of view with no invented person. Only a lead-in phrase that describes a real witness's own action (like H1 in Example 2, per Rule 7) may show that real witness. Never add a character the story never shows.

STORY MAPPING (script line 2 onward)

DO NOT rewrite or alter the voiceover script from Step 3. The spoken script MUST remain in complete, unbroken sentences, ending with proper punctuation.

Instead, mentally break down those complete sentences into ~3-second visual beats. Hook visuals are labeled [H1], [H2]… and story visuals are labeled [S1], [S2]… (two separate numberings).

You will output this breakdown in the "VISUAL SCENE MAP" in the Final Output (the hook breakdown is also printed in the HOOK BREAKDOWN section).

How to map it (MEANING-FIRST BEAT SLICING — NO DEAD FRAGMENTS):

1. SINGLE-ACTION SHORT/MEDIUM SENTENCES (2 to 10 words = ONE BEAT):
- If a complete sentence from Step 3 is 2 to 10 words AND describes ONE single continuous action or visual state, map the ENTIRE sentence to ONE single beat [S#].
- NEVER slice a 9- or 10-word sentence if slicing it would leave a "dead prepositional tail" (such as "...inside the freezing stone cell", "...for thirty-three years", or "...across the empty floor") that has no distinct visual action or new subject of its own.

Example (Short/Single-Action Sentence — Do NOT slice):
Spoken Sentence: "The city went SILENT."
Mapped into Visual Beat:
[S1] - "The city went SILENT."

Spoken Sentence: "One monk knelt alone inside the freezing stone CELL." (9 words, 1 single action)
Mapped into Visual Beat:
[S2] - "One monk knelt alone inside the freezing stone CELL."

2. LONGER OR TWO-PART SENTENCES (11 to 16 words, or 9–10 words with TWO distinct actions = TWO BEATS):
- Split sentences of 11 to 16 words (or 9–10 word sentences that contain two separate actions/clauses) into TWO beats at the most natural clause break, so each beat is roughly 4 to 8 words.
- SCRIPT-FIRST SLICING RULE: Both beats stay 100% faithful to the sentence's own words, and the two beats never produce the same shot:
     • If the sentence has TWO actions or subjects, Beat 1 shows the first and Beat 2 shows the second.
     • If the sentence describes ONE continuous action or state, both beats keep the script's true subject: Beat 1 shows the subject and action in a Wide or Medium shot; Beat 2 cuts to a Close-Up or a different angle of a part of that SAME subject or setting (a named person's face, hands, or eyes; the named object's surface or detail).
     • Never invent props, marks, or physical states the facts do not contain. Never show a blank wall or empty landscape unless the beat's own words describe that emptiness.

STRICT CUE GROUNDING RULE: Every CUE is one line of at most 12 words in this order: shot size — subject — its one frozen pose or key noun. Subjects, body parts, and objects in a CUE must come from this beat's spoken words, the previous beat of the same sentence, the CHARACTER SHEET, STORY WORLD, or (for abstract beats, Story Rule 7) one concrete object stated in the topic's facts. Plain supporting surfaces (floor, ground, the setting's own walls) are allowed for physical grounding. Never invent extra props, marks, or physical states (e.g., an unmentioned bag, paper, cracked screen, trembling hands, scratch marks).

Example Mapping Process (Two-Beat Sentence — Zero Invented Props):
Spoken Sentence (From Step 3): "Lisa had come to Athens to help people who had nowhere else to GO."
[S1] - "Lisa had come to Athens to help people..." | CUE: Wide — Lisa — walking down a narrow Athens street
[S2] - "...who had nowhere else to GO." | CUE: Medium — Lisa — facing a huddled group

Example Mapping Process (Two-Beat Sentence — Zero Invented Props):
Spoken Sentence (From Step 3): "Lisa had come to Athens to help people who had nowhere else to GO."
Mapped into Visual Beats (For the Visual Scene Map):
[S1] - "Lisa had come to Athens to help people..." | CUE: Lisa walking down a narrow Athens street in daylight
[S2] - "...who had nowhere else to GO." | CUE: Close shot of Lisa speaking gently to a huddled group

~3-Second Beats: Break the text down into natural beats that take roughly 1.5 to 3 seconds to narrate.

Continuous Flow: The script should not sound like a list of disconnected, isolated headlines. Each sentence must naturally bridge into the next beat to keep the viewer hooked.

Dynamic Visual Progression (Spatial Continuity): Every single beat must receive a unique visual prompt, but do NOT randomly jump locations in the middle of a continuous sentence.
CRITICAL: Even when maintaining the same location across multiple beats, you MUST still write out the full environment, lighting, and physics descriptions in every single prompt, exactly as required in Step 5. Never write "same as before."

STEP 5 — GENERATE VISUALS PROMPTS

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
These prompts make STILL IMAGES. In editing, each image gets a slow zoom or pan keyframe and stays on screen while its beat is spoken. Each image is generated alone, by a model that has never seen the script or any other prompt, so every prompt must be complete on its own. 

Before generating the image prompts, you must mentally map the Voiceover Script into visual beats using the HOOK MAPPING and STORY MAPPING rules given in Step 4. You will output this breakdown in the FINAL OUTPUT as the 'HOOK BREAKDOWN' and the 'VISUAL SCENE MAP'.

For each mapped visual moment, write one image prompt, built from its SUBJECT LOCK (hook) or its CUE (story).
 
⚠️ CRITICAL MAPPING RULE:
Before writing each prompt, ask: "What is LITERALLY happening in this exact beat RIGHT NOW?"
That answer = the visual.
NOT what happened before. NOT what comes next.
ONLY what this exact beat's SUBJECT LOCK (for hook) or CUE (for story) describes.
Never skip. Never combine. Never reorder.
 
⚠️ ACTION-VERB RULE (applies to EVERY prompt — hook and story):
Find the action word or key noun of the beat (waving, tapping, dragging, staring, burying, falling, a pale arm, a locked door…). The image must show EXACTLY that action — never a nearby, calmer, or different action. A still frame shows an action through ONE frozen pose, so before writing the prompt, choose the pose that can only mean that verb, for example:
• WAVING = hand raised, open palm facing the target, fingers spread, wrist tilted off vertical, slight blur on the fingertips — NOT pressed against, resting on, or touching anything.
• TAPPING = a knuckle or fingertip just making contact with the surface.
• DRAGGING = the body or object leaning against the pull, with a visible trail behind it.
• RUNNING / FALLING / REACHING / POINTING / any other verb = the unmistakable frozen pose of that verb.
The frozen pose must be in a form this subject can physically do (a lifeless body or corpse cannot act on its own and moves only by wind, water, or gravity). If the beat has no action word, the image shows its key noun large and clear. Never replace the beat's action with a resting or neutral pose. Write every prompt from its SUBJECT LOCK (for hook) or its CUE line (for story).
 
⚠️ CRITICAL TEXT RULE: Do NOT render any text, subtitles, captions, split-screen format, watermarks, or words inside the image. The image must be completely clean. If a story object itself carries letters or numbers (a carved name, tacks spelling initials), keep them small or out of focus.
 
CHARACTER SHEET (printed in the Final Output): for every person who appears, one fixed CHARACTER TAG of 12–20 words (age, build, skin tone, face, hair colour and length, clothing colour and fabric), taken from the topic's facts or, where the facts are silent, neutral period-accurate choices (used only in the images, never stated in the narration). Copy the tag word for word into every prompt that shows that person; change only what the story changes (e.g. "now gaunt, beard grown"). Face, hair and build never change. If a scene needs different clothing (indoors, at night), write that person's second tag once in the CHARACTER SHEET and copy it word for word in those scenes. Unnamed groups or crowds (e.g., "villagers", "search party", "passengers") do not get individual tags in the CHARACTER SHEET; describe them collectively in the beat with period-accurate clothing. Never use a name alone.

STORY WORLD (printed in the Final Output): 3–5 short facts taken only from the topic — era, location layout (what is inside, outside, above, below), and key props with the script line from which each exists or changes (line number and its first few words, e.g. "from line 6 ('By morning the trunk…'): the trunk sits sealed in the cellar"). Use a fact only in beats where it applies; a prop appears only when its beat needs it. Keep these facts to at most 5 short lines.
LOCATION TAGS (part of STORY WORLD): for every specific setting that appears in 3 or more beats, create a fixed LOCATION TAG of 12–20 words. Keep EXTERIOR and INTERIOR tags separate so exterior roofs/landscapes never leak into interior shots:
- For an EXTERIOR setting: building/structure shape, exterior wall material and colour, roof style, and immediate terrain/surroundings.
- For an INTERIOR setting: room scale, wall and floor material/colour, key fixed architectural feature (e.g., narrow barred window, stone hearth), and ambient lighting tone.
Copy the matching INTERIOR or EXTERIOR tag word for word into the [Environment] section of every Wide or Medium prompt set in that space. In Extreme Close-Up or Macro shots where the room or building is out of frame, do NOT paste the full room/building layout—use only the immediate contact surface and the tag's lighting and wall/surface colour tone so the camera stays tight on the close-up subject.


⚠️ HOOK VISUAL RULES — apply to H1…Hn only:
1. STRICT PHRASE COUNT: One prompt per hook phrase — never merge two phrases, never skip a phrase, never add an extra hook prompt. 
2. LITERAL PHRASE ACCURACY: Before writing each hook prompt, re-read the FULL hook line, then that one phrase. The image must LITERALLY show what that phrase says — same subject, same action, same moment. The action word must be visible as its frozen pose (ACTION-VERB RULE) that can only mean that verb, in a form this subject can physically do (a lifeless body moves only by wind, water, or gravity). Never swap it for a calmer or nearby action. 
3. SCROLL-STOPPING & CORE IMAGE LOCK: Hook visuals must be the strongest, most disturbing, mysterious, or curious images their phrases allow, and together they must cover the story's CORE IMAGE. The CORE IMAGE must be fully visible in at least one hook image (never off-camera or hidden). H1 must be the single most scroll-stopping image its phrase allows. A reaction image is allowed ONLY if the very next hook image shows what is being reacted to. All hook images must look like frames of the same reality. 
4. ONE MAIN SUBJECT & CENTRAL FRAMING: ONE main subject, large in the central 70% of the frame and readable in one second; state in plain words where it sits in the frame. 
5. RENDER-SAFE & VIEWPOINT CLARITY: Use physical arrangements an image generator has seen thousands of times (a coat hanging over a chair, a hand raised in front of a window, a small house alone in a field). Only ONE unusual element per image; everything else ordinary. Whenever glass, a window, a door, or water separates things, explicitly state where the camera is and which side every subject is on. Never describe a body part passing through gaps, holes, or planks; never "emerging from between" things; never place an object in an unusual orientation (e.g., a bed standing on its end); never use "swallowed by darkness". 
6. ZERO CLUTTER: Name nothing the phrase does not need. Any extra object you mention (a lamp, a rope, a bird) will be drawn prominently — leave it out unless it is the subject.
